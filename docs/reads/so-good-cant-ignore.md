## The core claim

> "Follow your passion" is bad advice. Skills trump passion in the quest for work you love.

Great work is built, not found. The traits of great work — creativity, impact, control — are rare and valuable, so you must offer something rare and valuable in exchange: your career capital.

## Glossary

### Passion Hypothesis

The claim that occupational happiness comes from first finding your passion, then matching a job to it.

### Passion Mindset

Asking "What can the world offer me?" — judging a job by what it gives you.

### Craftsman Mindset

Asking "What can I offer the world?" — focusing on becoming so good you can't be ignored. (p. 42)

### Career capital

Skills that are rare and valuable, traded for the traits of great work. (p. 48)

### Deliberate practice

Stretching just past your comfort zone on a defined skill, with fast feedback, sustained over time.

### Winner-take-all market

A field where one skill dominates all others — e.g. writing, stand-up, music. Optimise that one skill.

### Auction market

A field with many valuable skill types. Step through the open gates already available to you.

### Control

Autonomy over what you do and how you do it — "the dream-job elixir." (p. 113)

### Control Trap 1

Seeking control before you have the capital to back it. Control demands capital.

### Control Trap 2

Once you can earn control, your employer resists — because control benefits only you. Expect it.

### Law of Financial Viability

Before pursuing a control-gaining move, ask whether people will pay for it. If yes, continue; if no, 
move on.

### Mission

A unifying career goal, discovered at the frontier of your field, pursued through little bets and 
marketing. (p. 152)

### Adjacent possible

Compelling missions sit just beyond the cutting edge of your field — visible only once you're there.

### Little bets

Small, achievable projects that test whether a mission idea is real.

## The rules

1. Don't follow your passion. Passion is rare at the start and grows with competence.
2. Be so good they can't ignore you. Build career capital with deliberate practice.
3. Control is a reward, not a starting point. Earn it, then expect resistance.
4. Missions grow from the frontier. Little bets + marketing turn a mission into a success.

## Deliberate practice in five steps

1. Decide your capital market (winner-take-all or auction).
2. Identify your capital type (the dominant skill, or the open gate).
3. Define "good" — a specific, testable target.
4. Stretch and destroy — schedule discomfort deliberately.
5. Be patient — Steve Martin took decades. Diligence implies time.

## The two tests

### Control trajectory test

"Does this move give me more control, and did I earn it with capital — or am I grabbing it early?"

### Financial viability test: 

"Are people actually willing to pay for this pursuit? If not, it has no future."

## Ways to quit a job

Newport's red flags — jobs that hamper capital building: few opportunities to distinguish yourself; work you find useless or harmful; colleagues you really dislike.

## Passion Mindset vis-a-vis Craftsman mindset

> The passion mindset asks, "What can the world offer me?"
> 
> The craftsman mindset asks, "What can I offer the world?"

The book's argument: the passion mindset judges a job by what it gives you — so it makes you restless, job-hopping, and disappointed. The craftsman mindset judges your work by what you're becoming — rare, valuable skill — and the traits you want (creativity, impact, control) arrive as a reward, not a prerequisite. Passion is usually the outcome of competence, not its cause.

## Applied to leadership

The craftsman version of leadership asks a different question of each report: "What rare, valuable skill is this person building here — and is it a skill worth having?" That reframes every assignment, every 1:1, and every performance conversation. It also applies to you: are the hours you lead with building your own rare-and-valuable leadership skills, or just absorbing calendar time?
