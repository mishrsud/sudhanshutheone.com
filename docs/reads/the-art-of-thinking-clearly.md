**The Art of Thinking Clearly** by Rolf Dobelli is a collection of 99 short chapters, each examining a common cognitive bias, fallacy, or thinking error. Dobelli’s core message is that clearer thinking comes less from adding clever strategies and more from systematically avoiding these predictable traps. Below is a chapter-by-chapter summary in the requested format.

### 1. Why You Should Visit Cemeteries: Survivorship Bias
**Key insight:** We overestimate success odds because we only see the winners; the far larger number of failures remains invisible.  
- Actively seek out and study failures (the “cemeteries”).  
- Ask what the unsuccessful majority looked like before drawing conclusions from success stories.  
- Treat media success narratives with skepticism.

### 2. Does Harvard Make You Smarter?: Swimmer’s Body Illusion
**Key insight:** We confuse selection factors with results (swimmers have the body that makes them good at swimming; elite schools select the already talented).  
- Separate innate advantages or prior selection from the effects of training or education.  
- Question whether a prestigious institution or method actually causes the observed outcome.  
- Avoid copying surface traits of successful people without understanding selection.

### 3. Why You See Shapes in the Clouds: Clustering Illusion
**Key insight:** The brain is oversensitive to patterns and invents them even in pure randomness.  
- Default to “chance” when you spot a pattern until statistical evidence proves otherwise.  
- Demand proper statistical testing before acting on apparent correlations.  
- Remain skeptical of “hot streaks” or meaningful clusters in data.

### 4. If Fifty Million People Say Something Foolish, It Is Still Foolish: Social Proof
**Key insight:** We treat popularity as evidence of correctness (herd instinct).  
- Evaluate ideas on their own merits, not on how many people hold them.  
- Pause when you feel pressure to conform simply because others are doing something.  
- Be especially wary in uncertain or high-stakes situations.

### 5. Why You Should Forget the Past: Sunk Cost Fallacy
**Key insight:** Past investments (time, money, effort) should not dictate future decisions.  
- Evaluate only future costs and benefits.  
- Be willing to abandon projects once they no longer make sense, regardless of prior outlay.  
- Recognize the emotional pull of consistency and “not wasting” what is already gone.

### 6. Don’t Accept Free Drinks: Reciprocity
**Key insight:** We feel obligated to return favors even when they were unsolicited.  
- Recognize free gifts or favors as potential influence tactics.  
- Decide based on the actual value of what is being offered, not the sense of debt.  
- Politely decline unsolicited gifts when they create pressure.

### 7. Beware the “Special Case”: Confirmation Bias (Part 1)
**Key insight:** We interpret new information so that it fits existing beliefs and dismiss contradictory evidence.  
- Actively hunt for disconfirming evidence.  
- Treat “special cases” that rescue a theory with suspicion.  
- Write down core beliefs and deliberately challenge them.

### 8. Murder Your Darlings: Confirmation Bias (Part 2)
**Key insight:** We cling to cherished ideas even after they have been disproven.  
- Be willing to kill favorite theories or strategies when evidence turns against them.  
- Seek out people and sources that disagree with you.  
- Practice changing your mind publicly when warranted.

### 9. Don’t Bow to Authority: Authority Bias
**Key insight:** We give excessive weight to the opinions of authority figures.  
- Evaluate the content of the advice, not the status of the speaker.  
- Ask what evidence or track record supports the authority’s claim.  
- Create environments where subordinates can safely challenge superiors.

### 10. Leave Your Supermodel Friends at Home: Contrast Effect
**Key insight:** Judgments are distorted by what we compare things against.  
- Judge options in isolation or against relevant benchmarks, not extreme contrasts.  
- Be aware that a mediocre option looks better next to a poor one (and vice versa).  
- Avoid sequential presentations designed to manipulate relative attractiveness.

### 11. Why We Prefer a Wrong Map to None at All: Availability Bias
**Key insight:** We construct our picture of the world from whatever comes most easily to mind.  
- Seek base rates and less vivid but more representative data.  
- Spend time with people whose experiences differ from yours.  
- Question whether vivid examples are actually typical.

### 12. Why “No Pain, No Gain” Should Set Alarm Bells Ringing: The It’ll-Get-Worse-Before-It-Gets-Better Fallacy
**Key insight:** We accept temporary deterioration as proof that a strategy is working, even when it is not.  
- Demand clear metrics that distinguish necessary short-term pain from failure.  
- Set decision rules in advance for when to abandon a worsening course.  
- Be wary of advisors who use this narrative to buy time.

### 13. Even True Stories Are Fairy Tales: Story Bias
**Key insight:** We prefer coherent stories to messy reality and therefore overlook omitted facts.  
- Look for what has been left out of any neat narrative.  
- Prefer statistical distributions over single anecdotes.  
- Resist the urge to force events into a clean cause-and-effect story.

### 14. Why You Should Keep a Diary: Hindsight Bias
**Key insight:** After an event occurs, we believe we “knew it all along.”  
- Keep contemporaneous records of predictions and reasoning.  
- Review past forecasts honestly rather than rewriting them.  
- Use the diary to calibrate your actual forecasting skill.

### 15. Why You Systematically Overestimate Your Knowledge and Abilities: Overconfidence Effect
**Key insight:** We systematically overestimate our knowledge, skills, and predictive accuracy.  
- Seek external feedback and objective measures.  
- Express confidence intervals rather than point estimates.  
- Assume you know less than you think, especially in complex domains.

### 16. Don’t Take News Anchors Seriously: Chauffeur Knowledge
**Key insight:** We confuse fluent presentation with genuine expertise.  
- Distinguish “knowing how to talk about something” from deep understanding.  
- Probe for real knowledge rather than polished talking points.  
- Prefer specialists who can admit limits over smooth generalists.

### 17. You Control Less Than You Think: Illusion of Control
**Key insight:** We overestimate our influence over outcomes that are largely random or external.  
- Distinguish what is truly under your control from what is not.  
- Focus effort on the controllable factors.  
- Accept that many results are driven by chance or larger systems.

### 18. Never Pay Your Lawyer by the Hour: Incentive Super-Response Tendency
**Key insight:** People respond strongly (and often perversely) to the incentives they face.  
- Align incentives with desired outcomes.  
- Anticipate how others will game the system.  
- Prefer fixed-fee or outcome-based arrangements where possible.

### 19. The Dubious Efficacy of Doctors, Consultants and Psychotherapists: Regression to the Mean
**Key insight:** Extreme results tend to be followed by more average ones; we misattribute the improvement.  
- Expect regression after unusually good or bad performance.  
- Do not credit interventions for natural reversion to the mean.  
- Use control groups or longer baselines when evaluating effectiveness.

### 20. Never Judge a Decision by Its Outcome: Outcome Bias
**Key insight:** We judge the quality of a decision by its result rather than by the information available at the time.  
- Evaluate decisions on process and information quality, not hindsight results.  
- Separate luck from skill when reviewing outcomes.  
- Avoid punishing good decisions that happened to turn out poorly.

### 21. Less Is More: The Paradox of Choice
**Key insight:** More options often lead to more anxiety, paralysis, and lower satisfaction.  
- Limit the number of alternatives you consider.  
- Decide on decision criteria first, then choose.  
- Accept “good enough” rather than endlessly searching for the optimal.

### 22. You Like Me, You Really Really Like Me: Liking Bias
**Key insight:** We favor people we like and are more easily influenced by them.  
- Separate personal liking from the quality of the proposal or product.  
- Be aware when sales or persuasion tactics rely on rapport.  
- Make important decisions based on merits, not affinity.

### 23. Don’t Cling to Things: Endowment Effect
**Key insight:** We value things more highly simply because we own them.  
- Imagine you do not own the item when deciding whether to keep or sell it.  
- Use market prices rather than personal attachment as the guide.  
- Be willing to part with possessions that no longer serve you.

### 24. The Inevitability of Unlikely Events: Coincidence
**Key insight:** Unlikely events happen regularly simply because so many opportunities exist.  
- Do not over-interpret coincidences as meaningful.  
- Remember the law of large numbers in everyday life.  
- Avoid assigning causal stories to random concurrent events.

### 25. The Calamity of Conformity: Groupthink
**Key insight:** Groups suppress dissent and converge on suboptimal decisions to maintain harmony.  
- Actively encourage devil’s advocates and dissenting views.  
- Structure decision processes to surface disagreement.  
- Value independent judgment over group consensus for its own sake.

### 26. Why You’ll Soon Be Playing Megatrillions: Neglect of Probability
**Key insight:** We ignore or poorly weight actual probabilities, especially for rare events.  
- Explicitly estimate base rates and probabilities.  
- Do not let the emotional vividness of an outcome override its likelihood.  
- Use expected-value thinking for significant decisions.

### 27. Why the Last Cookie in the Jar Makes Your Mouth Water: Scarcity Error
**Key insight:** We value things more when they appear scarce or limited.  
- Ask whether scarcity is real or manufactured.  
- Decide based on absolute value, not relative rarity.  
- Resist urgency tactics that play on fear of missing out.

### 28. When You Hear Hoofbeats, Don’t Expect a Zebra: Base-Rate Neglect
**Key insight:** We ignore general statistical frequencies in favor of specific case details.  
- Always start with the base rate of the category.  
- Update with case-specific information only after establishing the prior.  
- Beware of vivid stories that make rare events seem common.

### 29. Why the “Balancing Force of the Universe” Is Baloney: Gambler’s Fallacy
**Key insight:** We believe that past random outcomes influence future independent ones.  
- Treat independent events as independent.  
- Do not expect “due” outcomes after streaks.  
- Reset expectations after every independent trial.

### 30. Why the Wheel of Fortune Makes Our Heads Spin: The Anchor
**Key insight:** Arbitrary numbers or first pieces of information heavily influence subsequent judgments.  
- Generate your own independent estimate before seeing anchors.  
- Be aware of opening offers and suggested prices.  
- Adjust deliberately away from the first number presented.

### 31. How to Relieve People of Their Millions: Induction
**Key insight:** We extrapolate too confidently from limited past observations.  
- Recognize that past patterns can break.  
- Prefer theories with explanatory power over pure induction.  
- Remain open to black-swan events that invalidate prior trends.

### 32. Why Evil Strikes Harder Than Good: Loss Aversion
**Key insight:** Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please us.  
- Frame decisions in terms of overall wealth or long-term outcomes.  
- Be willing to accept small, certain losses to avoid larger risks.  
- Notice when loss aversion is keeping you stuck.

### 33. Why Teams Are Lazy: Social Loafing
**Key insight:** Individuals reduce effort when working in groups because responsibility is diffused.  
- Make individual contributions measurable and visible.  
- Keep teams small when possible.  
- Assign clear personal accountability.

### 34. Stumped by a Sheet of Paper: Exponential Growth
**Key insight:** We chronically underestimate the power of exponential processes.  
- Learn to calculate and visualize exponential curves.  
- Apply the concept to debt, population, technology, and compounding.  
- Do not rely on linear intuition for growth phenomena.

### 35. Curb Your Enthusiasm: Winner’s Curse
**Key insight:** The winner of an auction or competitive bid often overpays.  
- Bid conservatively and account for the winner’s curse.  
- Set a maximum value in advance and stick to it.  
- Prefer sealed or carefully structured bidding processes.

### 36. Never Ask a Writer If the Novel Is Autobiographical: Fundamental Attribution Error
**Key insight:** We over-attribute behavior to personality and under-attribute it to situations.  
- Consider situational pressures before judging character.  
- Apply the same charitable interpretation to others that you apply to yourself.  
- Look for external constraints when explaining actions.

### 37. Why You Shouldn’t Believe in the Stork: False Causality
**Key insight:** We confuse correlation with causation.  
- Ask what third factors or reverse causality might explain the link.  
- Prefer controlled experiments or strong causal designs.  
- Be skeptical of causal claims based solely on observed association.

### 38. Everyone Is Beautiful at the Top: Halo Effect
**Key insight:** One positive trait colors our overall judgment of a person or product.  
- Evaluate attributes independently.  
- Separate appearance, charisma, or status from competence.  
- Use structured criteria rather than global impressions.

### 39. Congratulations! You’ve Won Russian Roulette: Alternative Paths
**Key insight:** We ignore the many paths that could have led to different outcomes and focus only on the one that occurred.  
- Consider the full distribution of possible results, not just the realized one.  
- Ask “what else could have happened?”  
- Avoid survivorship-style storytelling about single successful paths.

### 40. False Prophets: Forecast Illusion
**Key insight:** Experts and non-experts alike are poor at forecasting complex events, yet we over-rely on predictions.  
- Treat most forecasts with heavy skepticism.  
- Prefer ranges and scenarios over precise point predictions.  
- Evaluate forecasters by track record, not confidence or credentials alone.

### 41. The Deception of Specific Cases: Conjunction Fallacy
**Key insight:** We judge a more specific combination of events as more probable than a single broader event.  
- Remember that adding details can only decrease or keep probability the same.  
- Prefer the more general statement when assessing likelihood.  
- Watch for stories that feel more “representative” but are less probable.

### 42. It’s Not What You Say, but How You Say It: Framing
**Key insight:** The same information presented differently leads to different decisions.  
- Reframe problems in multiple ways (gains vs. losses, percentages vs. absolute numbers).  
- Look for the underlying facts independent of presentation.  
- Be alert to framing used in marketing, politics, and negotiations.

### 43. Why Watching and Waiting Is Torture: Action Bias
**Key insight:** In uncertain situations we feel compelled to act even when inaction is better.  
- Default to “do nothing” unless action has a clear positive expected value.  
- Distinguish activity from progress.  
- Create deliberate waiting periods for important decisions.

### 44. Why You Are Either the Solution – or the Problem: Omission Bias
**Key insight:** We judge harmful actions more harshly than equally harmful inactions.  
- Evaluate the consequences of both acting and not acting.  
- Do not let the moral weight of commission versus omission distort expected-value calculations.  
- Accept responsibility for outcomes of deliberate inaction.

### 45. Don’t Blame Me: Self-Serving Bias
**Key insight:** We attribute successes to ourselves and failures to external factors.  
- Seek objective feedback and third-party perspectives.  
- Conduct pre-mortems and post-mortems that examine your own contribution.  
- Practice attributing both good and bad outcomes more even-handedly.

### 46. Be Careful What You Wish For: Hedonic Treadmill
**Key insight:** We quickly adapt to improved circumstances and return to a baseline level of happiness.  
- Pursue experiences and relationships that resist adaptation more than material gains.  
- Lower expectations rather than constantly raising the bar.  
- Focus on process and present enjoyment rather than permanent elevation of mood.

### 47. Do Not Marvel at Your Existence: Self-Selection Bias
**Key insight:** We observe only the situations we are in and therefore misjudge probabilities.  
- Recognize that your presence itself selects the sample you see.  
- Adjust for the fact that you only experience the paths that led to your current position.  
- Use broader data rather than personal existence as evidence.

### 48. Why Experience Can Damage Our Judgement: Association Bias
**Key insight:** We form associations that are not causal and then act on them.  
- Test whether the association is causal or merely correlational.  
- Avoid letting incidental pairings (place, person, timing) dictate future decisions.  
- Update beliefs when associations prove unreliable.

### 49. Be Wary When Things Get Off to a Great Start: Beginner’s Luck
**Key insight:** Early success is often due to chance and can create overconfidence.  
- Treat initial wins as provisional.  
- Continue rigorous evaluation even after early positive results.  
- Avoid scaling strategies solely on the basis of beginner’s luck.

### 50. Sweet Little Lies: Cognitive Dissonance
**Key insight:** We rationalize conflicting beliefs or actions to reduce psychological discomfort.  
- Notice when you are generating justifications after the fact.  
- Prefer changing behavior or beliefs over elaborate rationalization.  
- Invite external challenge to your post-hoc stories.

### 51. Live Each Day as If It Were Your Last – But Only on Sundays: Hyperbolic Discounting
**Key insight:** We heavily discount future rewards relative to immediate ones, inconsistently.  
- Use commitment devices and pre-commitments.  
- Make future consequences more vivid and immediate.  
- Prefer rules and systems over relying on momentary willpower.

### 52. Any Lame Excuse: “Because” Justification
**Key insight:** Providing any reason (even a weak one) increases compliance.  
- Distinguish real reasons from placeholder justifications.  
- Demand substantive explanations.  
- Be aware of the power of the word “because” in persuasion.

### 53. Decide Better – Decide Less: Decision Fatigue
**Key insight:** The quality of decisions declines after a series of choices.  
- Make important decisions early in the day or after rest.  
- Automate or reduce low-stakes decisions.  
- Preserve mental energy for high-impact choices.

### 54. Would You Wear Hitler’s Sweater?: Contagion Bias
**Key insight:** We treat objects as carrying the essence of people or events associated with them.  
- Separate symbolic contamination from actual properties.  
- Evaluate items on functional merits rather than history alone.  
- Recognize the emotional power of contamination narratives.

### 55. Why There Is No Such Thing as an Average War: The Problem with Averages
**Key insight:** Averages can hide important variation and non-linear effects.  
- Look at distributions, not just means.  
- Be cautious when the average is not representative of typical cases.  
- Consider extremes and tails when they matter.

### 56. How Bonuses Destroy Motivation: Motivation Crowding
**Key insight:** Extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic motivation.  
- Use monetary incentives carefully, especially for creative or pro-social work.  
- Preserve autonomy and purpose where possible.  
- Monitor whether incentives are undermining the very behavior they aim to encourage.

### 57. If You Have Nothing to Say, Say Nothing: Twaddle Tendency
**Key insight:** We produce and accept empty, complex language that sounds intelligent but says little.  
- Prefer clear, simple expression.  
- Challenge jargon and vague statements.  
- Value substance over sophisticated-sounding fluff.

### 58. How to Increase the Average IQ of Two States: Will Rogers Phenomenon
**Key insight:** Moving items between groups can improve averages in both groups without any real improvement.  
- Watch for reclassification effects when evaluating group statistics.  
- Examine composition changes before celebrating average gains.  
- Prefer absolute measures over relative group averages when possible.

### 59. If You Have an Enemy, Give Him Information: Information Bias
**Key insight:** We seek information even when it will not affect the decision.  
- Ask whether additional data will change the action.  
- Stop gathering information once the decision threshold is reached.  
- Distinguish decision-relevant information from interesting but useless data.

### 60. Hurts So Good: Effort Justification
**Key insight:** We value outcomes more highly when we have suffered to achieve them.  
- Evaluate results independently of the effort expended.  
- Avoid escalating commitment simply because the path was hard.  
- Recognize that pain does not automatically confer value.

### 61. Why Small Things Loom Large: The Law of Small Numbers
**Key insight:** We over-interpret results from small samples.  
- Demand larger samples before drawing strong conclusions.  
- Expect more volatility in small groups or short time periods.  
- Treat anecdotal or tiny-n findings as provisional.

### 62. Handle with Care: Expectations
**Key insight:** Expectations shape both perception and actual outcomes (placebo, self-fulfilling prophecy).  
- Manage expectations consciously.  
- Be aware of how your expectations influence others’ performance.  
- Test whether results are driven by the intervention or by expectation effects.

### 63. Speed Traps Ahead!: Simple Logic
**Key insight:** We prefer simple explanations and solutions even when the problem is complex.  
- Resist oversimplification of multifaceted issues.  
- Accept that some problems require nuanced, multi-factor approaches.  
- Prefer accurate complexity over elegant but wrong simplicity when stakes are high.

### 64. How to Expose a Charlatan: Forer Effect
**Key insight:** We accept vague, general personality descriptions as uniquely accurate.  
- Demand specific, falsifiable claims.  
- Test whether the description would fit almost anyone.  
- Be skeptical of horoscopes, generic profiles, and flattering assessments.

### 65. Volunteer Work Is for the Birds: Volunteer’s Folly
**Key insight:** Unpaid work is not automatically more valuable or virtuous than paid alternatives.  
- Compare opportunity costs of volunteering versus earning and donating.  
- Value effectiveness over the feeling of contribution.  
- Choose high-impact uses of time and money.

### 66. Why You Are a Slave to Your Emotions: Affect Heuristic
**Key insight:** Emotional reactions substitute for careful analysis of risks and benefits.  
- Separate emotional response from analytical evaluation.  
- Use structured tools when feelings are strong.  
- Notice when liking or disliking is driving the judgment.

### 67. Be Your Own Heretic: Introspection Illusion
**Key insight:** We overestimate the accuracy of our own self-insight and undervalue external evidence.  
- Treat your own introspections as hypotheses, not facts.  
- Seek behavioral evidence and outside perspectives.  
- Be more skeptical of your own motives than of others’.

### 68. Why You Should Set Fire to Your Ships: Inability to Close Doors
**Key insight:** We keep options open even when doing so is costly.  
- Deliberately close low-value options to free attention and resources.  
- Commit fully when the expected value is clear.  
- Recognize the hidden cost of optionality.

### 69. Disregard the Brand New: Neomania
**Key insight:** We overvalue novelty simply because it is new.  
- Evaluate innovations on actual benefits, not newness.  
- Prefer proven solutions unless the new option clearly outperforms.  
- Resist fashion-driven adoption.

### 70. Why Propaganda Works: Sleeper Effect
**Key insight:** The influence of a message can grow over time as we forget the untrustworthy source.  
- Record the source of information along with the content.  
- Re-evaluate claims when the source is later revealed as biased.  
- Be aware that discounted messages can still shape attitudes later.

### 71. Why It’s Never Just a Two-Horse Race: Alternative Blindness
**Key insight:** We focus on the options presented and ignore better alternatives that were not listed.  
- Explicitly generate additional options.  
- Ask “what else could we do?”  
- Expand the choice set before deciding.

### 72. Why We Take Aim at Young Guns: Social Comparison Bias
**Key insight:** We feel threatened by talented newcomers and may undermine them.  
- Separate personal status concerns from organizational or team benefit.  
- Actively support high performers even when they outshine you.  
- Measure success by collective results rather than relative standing.

### 73. Why First Impressions Deceive: Primacy and Recency Effects
**Key insight:** Items presented first or last receive disproportionate weight.  
- Be aware of order effects in lists, interviews, and presentations.  
- Review information multiple times in different sequences.  
- Weight middle items more carefully.

### 74. Why You Can’t Beat Home-Made: Not-Invented-Here Syndrome
**Key insight:** We undervalue ideas that originate outside our own group.  
- Evaluate external ideas on merit, not origin.  
- Actively seek and reward outside solutions.  
- Create processes that overcome internal bias against external innovation.

### 75. How to Profit from the Implausible: The Black Swan
**Key insight:** Rare, high-impact events are both more consequential and less predictable than we assume.  
- Build robustness and optionality against extreme events.  
- Do not rely solely on historical frequencies for tail risks.  
- Position yourself to benefit from positive black swans while limiting downside.

### 76. Knowledge Is Non-Transferable: Domain Dependence
**Key insight:** Expertise in one domain often fails to transfer to another.  
- Do not assume skill in one area automatically confers skill elsewhere.  
- Re-learn and re-test when moving domains.  
- Value domain-specific experience.

### 77. The Myth of Like-Mindedness: False-Consensus Effect
**Key insight:** We overestimate how much others share our beliefs and preferences.  
- Actively sample outside your usual circles.  
- Test assumptions about what “everyone” thinks.  
- Design for diversity of opinion rather than assumed consensus.

### 78. You Were Right All Along: Falsification of History
**Key insight:** We rewrite our past beliefs and predictions to appear more consistent with what actually happened.  
- Keep contemporaneous records.  
- Compare current recollections with original statements.  
- Accept that your past self was often wrong.

### 79. Why You Identify with Your Football Team: In-Group Out-Group Bias
**Key insight:** We favor our own group and denigrate outsiders, often on arbitrary grounds.  
- Recognize the arbitrary nature of many group boundaries.  
- Apply the same standards to in-group and out-group members.  
- Seek contact and cooperation across groups.

### 80. The Difference Between Risk and Uncertainty: Ambiguity Aversion
**Key insight:** We prefer known risks over unknown uncertainties even when the unknown may be better.  
- Distinguish measurable risk from true uncertainty.  
- Do not automatically prefer the known quantity.  
- Develop methods for acting under genuine uncertainty.

### 81. Why You Go with the Status Quo: Default Effect
**Key insight:** We stick with pre-selected options far more than is rational.  
- Treat defaults as choices that still require evaluation.  
- Design defaults carefully when you set them for others.  
- Periodically review whether the status quo remains optimal.

### 82. Why “Last Chances” Make Us Panic: Fear of Regret
**Key insight:** Anticipated regret drives many decisions, often suboptimally.  
- Focus on expected value rather than possible future regret.  
- Accept that some regret is inevitable.  
- Make decisions you can defend to your future self on process grounds.

### 83. How Eye-Catching Details Render Us Blind: Salience Effect
**Key insight:** Vivid, attention-grabbing features dominate judgment while more important but less noticeable factors are ignored.  
- Deliberately look for less salient but higher-impact variables.  
- Use checklists to force attention to quiet factors.  
- Discount dramatic details that are statistically minor.

### 84. Why Money Is Not Naked: House-Money Effect
**Key insight:** We treat money differently depending on how we obtained it (more carelessly with “found” or won money).  
- Treat all money as fungible.  
- Make decisions based on total wealth, not mental accounts.  
- Avoid riskier behavior with windfall gains.

### 85. Why New Year’s Resolutions Don’t Work: Procrastination
**Key insight:** We delay aversive tasks even when the delay is costly.  
- Break tasks into smaller, immediate next actions.  
- Use deadlines, accountability, and environmental design.  
- Reduce the friction of starting.

### 86. Build Your Own Castle: Envy
**Key insight:** Comparing ourselves to others generates unhappiness and poor decisions.  
- Focus on your own absolute progress rather than relative standing.  
- Limit exposure to envy-inducing comparisons.  
- Celebrate others’ success without measuring yourself against it.

### 87. Why You Prefer Novels to Statistics: Personification
**Key insight:** We respond more strongly to individual stories than to statistical aggregates.  
- Balance personal stories with base-rate data.  
- Use both narrative and numbers.  
- Be aware when a single vivid case is being used to override broader evidence.

### 88. You Have No Idea What You Are Overlooking: Illusion of Attention
**Key insight:** We believe we notice far more of our environment than we actually do.  
- Accept the severe limits of attention.  
- Design systems and environments that do not rely on continuous vigilance.  
- Use external checks for important details.

### 89. Hot Air: Strategic Misrepresentation
**Key insight:** People deliberately overstate benefits and understate costs to get projects approved.  
- Apply independent scrutiny to forecasts and budgets.  
- Build in buffers and contingency for optimistic projections.  
- Hold proposers accountable for earlier estimates.

### 90. Where’s the Off Switch?: Overthinking
**Key insight:** Excessive analysis can paralyze action or degrade decision quality.  
- Set decision deadlines.  
- Distinguish reversible from irreversible choices and act faster on the former.  
- Stop gathering information once further data will not change the choice.

### 91. Why You Take On Too Much: Planning Fallacy
**Key insight:** We systematically underestimate the time, costs, and risks of future tasks.  
- Use reference-class forecasting (how long similar projects actually took).  
- Add explicit buffers.  
- Break plans into smaller parts and track early progress.

### 92. Those Wielding Hammers See Only Nails: Déformation Professionnelle
**Key insight:** Professional training causes us to see every problem through the lens of our specialty.  
- Consult people from other disciplines.  
- Question whether your preferred tool is actually the right one.  
- Expand your toolkit deliberately.

### 93. Mission Accomplished: Zeigarnik Effect
**Key insight:** Unfinished tasks occupy more mental space than completed ones.  
- Capture open loops in a trusted system.  
- Complete or deliberately abandon tasks to free attention.  
- Use the effect positively by starting tasks to create psychological pull.

### 94. The Boat Matters More Than the Rowing: Illusion of Skill
**Key insight:** In domains dominated by chance, we over-attribute results to skill.  
- Identify the relative contributions of skill versus luck.  
- Do not overpay for perceived skill in high-randomness fields.  
- Focus skill development where it actually matters.

### 95. Why Checklists Deceive You: Feature-Positive Effect
**Key insight:** We notice presence more readily than absence.  
- Explicitly check for missing features or non-events.  
- Use structured lists that force attention to what is not there.  
- Be wary of conclusions drawn only from positive instances.

### 96. Drawing the Bull’s-Eye Around the Arrow: Cherry-Picking
**Key insight:** We select evidence that supports a preferred conclusion and ignore the rest.  
- Examine the full set of relevant data.  
- Pre-specify what would count as confirming or disconfirming evidence.  
- Be suspicious of post-hoc selection of favorable cases.

### 97. The Stone-Age Hunt for Scapegoats: Fallacy of the Single Cause
**Key insight:** We look for one simple cause when outcomes usually have multiple interacting causes.  
- Map multiple contributing factors.  
- Avoid single-cause explanations for complex events.  
- Design interventions that address several drivers.

### 98. Speed Demons Make Safe Drivers: Intention-to-Treat Error
**Key insight:** We evaluate interventions based on those who completed them rather than those originally assigned.  
- Analyze by original intention or assignment.  
- Account for dropouts and non-compliance.  
- Prefer intention-to-treat analyses for real-world effectiveness.

### 99. Why You Shouldn’t Read the News: News Illusion
**Key insight:** News focuses on the dramatic, immediate, and exceptional, distorting our picture of reality.  
- Limit news consumption.  
- Prefer slower, deeper sources and base-rate information.  
- Recognize that most news is irrelevant to long-term decisions and well-being.
